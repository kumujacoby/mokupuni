1st Grade · Engineering & Design

Mokupuni

A Communal Island Build · 5 Sessions

Grade1st Grade
Sessions5 × 30 min
MaterialsKnex · LEGO Minifigures
Build SurfaceFLL Regulation Table
Classes4 Classes · Shared Island

Four classes. One island. Built together across a quarter — without ever all being in the same room. Each class inherits the island as others left it, adding their own places and people, stewarding what came before. The Knex builds the land. The minifigures bring it to life. The conversations make it meaningful.

Day 1 Lāhui Community / People

We Are Community

Set the stage for the whole unit. Students learn what a mokupuni is, agree on how they will care for the shared island, and understand that other classes will be building alongside them all quarter.

Step-by-Step
  1. Gather students together before approaching the table. Ask: "What is a mokupuni?" Let students share. Introduce the concept of an island as a place where everything is connected — the mountains, the ocean, the plants, the people.
  2. Reveal the FLL table and the Knex and minifigure materials. Tell students this is where their island will be built — and that it will grow every time any 1st grade class visits this room.
  3. Ask students: "If we are all going to build on the same island, what rules do we need?" Facilitate a short discussion and land on 3–4 agreements together. Write them large and post them at the table where they will stay all quarter.
  4. Close with the most important idea: other classes will build here too. Students may see things added that they didn't make. Their job is to respect and care for all of it — even the parts made by people they don't know.
  5. No building today. Let the anticipation build. End with: "Next time we come, the island begins."
Tips & Reminders

Key question to revisit: "What does an island community need to survive and thrive?" Push beyond basic answers — food and water, yes, but also people, relationships, places to gather.

Community agreements should be student language, not yours. If they say "be nice," ask them what that looks like when someone accidentally knocks something over. Get specific.

No building today is intentional. The restraint creates investment. If students ask why they can't start yet, tell them Day 1 is about becoming a community before building one.

Day 2 I Mua Move Forward

We Begin

First build session. Students observe what's already on the island before touching anything, then begin building their own places and choosing their minifigure.

Step-by-Step
  1. Before students approach the table, pause them. Ask: "What do you think you might find on the island today?" Let a few students guess. Then bring them to the table.
  2. Give students 2–3 minutes of quiet observation. No touching yet. Ask: "What do you notice? What was left for us? What does it make you think about?" Take a few responses.
  3. Remind students of the community agreements posted at the table. Ask a student to read them aloud.
  4. Open the build. Let students choose their area and begin. Students may also choose a minifigure at this point — this becomes their person on the island.
  5. Circulate and ask connective questions: "What is this place?" "What happens here?" "How does your place connect to what's already there?" Avoid directing — ask and listen.
  6. Give a 3-minute warning before cleanup. Students place their minifigure somewhere on or near their build and step back. Close with: "The island keeps growing. Next time, something new will be here."
Tips & Reminders

The observation moment is important. Don't skip it, even if students are eager to build. It builds the habit of noticing and respecting what came before.

Knex is abstract by design. When a student builds something that could be anything, ask them to tell you what it is rather than guessing. Their explanation is the learning.

If something gets knocked over: refer to the community agreements calmly. "What do we do when that happens?" Help them find the answer in their own rules.

Minifigure choice: Students pick one figure and it stays theirs for the unit. If figures are in a bin, let students browse briefly before building starts.

Day 3 Hoʻomau Persevere / Continue

We Respond

Continue building with an eye toward what others have added. A mid-session pause invites students to notice what's changed and name their minifigure's story.

Step-by-Step
  1. Bring students to the table. Ask: "What changed since last time we were here?" Give them a moment to look carefully before answering.
  2. Ask: "How do you feel about what you see? Is there anything you want to take care of or add to?" Validate curiosity and even mild frustration — this is real community work.
  3. Remind students of agreements. Open the build.
  4. About halfway through, call a brief 60-second pause. Ask students to look up from their own area and notice the island as a whole. Name something interesting you see.
  5. Encourage students to place their minifigure and share one sentence: "My person's name is ___ and they live ___."
  6. Cleanup and close. Ask one student to share something they added today and why.
Tips & Reminders

The mid-session pause is worth protecting. It shifts attention from "my build" to "our island." Keep it short but make it happen.

If a student is upset that something they made was changed, acknowledge it genuinely. Then ask: "How could we leave a signal for the next class about what's important here?"

Minifigure stories don't need to be elaborate. One sentence is enough. The goal is for each student to have a named person in a specific place.

Day 4 Mālama Care / Protect

We Steward

Students shift attention from their own corner to the health of the whole island. The focus is on relationships between places — and between communities.

Step-by-Step
  1. Opening observation. "What do you notice today? What's new? What's the same?"
  2. Before building, introduce today's focus: "Every part of an island needs something from another part. The mountains need rain. The fishponds need fresh water. The people need food from the land and the sea."
  3. Give students this prompt before they build: "What does your place need from another place on the island?" Let it sit as they work.
  4. Circulate with connective questions: "Who lives near you?" "What do they share?" "If something happened to your place, what else would be affected?"
  5. Begin planting the seed for Day 5: "If you had to explain your place to someone who has never seen it, what would you say?"
  6. Cleanup and close. Ask the group: "What is one thing you want to make sure is still here on our last day?"
Tips & Reminders

Mālama means to care for. If the moment feels right, use the word with students. "You're mālama-ing the island right now."

Relationships between places is the big idea today. Push students to think beyond their own build. "Look around. What's near your place? What do those people share with yours?"

Day 5 prep: Planting the seed today ("what will you share next time?") makes the Hoʻike session run much more smoothly.

Day 5 Hoʻike To Show / Demonstrate

We Share

No new building. Students share the story of their place and their person, then step back to reflect on the whole island — including the parts they didn't make.

Step-by-Step
  1. Gather students at the table. Tell them: "Today we're not building. Today we're sharing." Let that land.
  2. Each student finds their minifigure and shares: their person's name, where they live on the island, and one thing their person does or needs.
  3. After everyone has shared, zoom out. Ask: "What do you see that you didn't make?" "What surprised you?" "What's something another class built that you think is really interesting?"
  4. Name the big idea: "Some of this island was made by students in other classes who are never in this room with you. You built alongside people you've never met. That's what it means to share a place. That's what it means to be lāhui."
  5. Optional closing: ask students what they would want the next class to know about their part of the island.
Tips & Reminders

Keep sharing low-stakes. Holding up a minifigure and saying a few sentences is enough. The goal is voice, not performance.

The "parts I didn't make" moment is the most important part of Day 5. Don't rush past it. Let students sit with the wonder of a world they helped build but don't fully understand.

Lāhui — bring it back to the word from Day 1. "We started by talking about lāhui. Look at what lāhui built."

The island stays up between sessions. Handle the table and materials with care so other classes can still experience their Hoʻike.

Facilitation Notes

On the Shared Island

Students will encounter choices made by other classes without context. Resist the urge to explain. Let them sit with not knowing — and honor it anyway. This is the most important lesson of the unit.

On Knex + Minifigures

Knex builds the landforms at scale, quickly. Minifigures anchor the human story. Because Knex is abstract, students must explain what their structures represent — that explanatory moment is where the learning lives.

On Accidental Destruction

It will happen. Have a clear, non-punitive norm from Day 1: if you knock something down, you help fix it. Frame it as part of being a good community member, not a disciplinary moment.

On the First Builder

The first class in each cycle sets the tone everyone else responds to. Over time you can name it: "You're building first this week. What will you leave for the next community?"

On ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi

The day titles are the primary language touchpoints. Use them naturally — say the word, say what it means, move on. The goal is for the words to feel like they belong here, because they do.

On Transitions

30 minutes goes fast. Protect the opening observation moment every session. Aim for 3 minutes of looking, 20 minutes of building, 5 minutes of cleanup and close.
